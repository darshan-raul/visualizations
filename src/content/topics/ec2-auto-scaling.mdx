## One group, several versions of capacity

Suppose an Auto Scaling group has six instances across three Availability Zones. Traffic rises, so a target-tracking policy raises desired capacity to eight. That number changes immediately. Usable capacity does not: EC2 still has to accept two launches, the instances must boot, lifecycle work may need to finish, and the targets must become ready.

Now let one existing target fail while another instance is protected from scale in. The group is handling a scale-out request, a health replacement, and an eligibility constraint at the same time. Looking only at **desired capacity** or the green EC2 `running` badge hides most of the answer.

## The timers have different owners

Auto Scaling timing settings sit close together in the console, but they do not pause the same action. An ELB target keeps receiving health checks during an ASG health-check grace period. Grace changes when the group acts on a startup health failure. It does not suppress the raw checks, and leaving EC2 `running` can still lead to immediate replacement.

Warmup controls eligibility for scaling calculations and refresh progress. It does not erase CloudWatch samples or decide when a target begins receiving traffic. A simple-scaling cooldown gates another simple-scaling action; it does not lock health replacement, schedules, manual changes, target tracking, or step scaling.

A termination hook and target deregistration delay are separate clocks. In the ordinary flow, draining begins before the termination hook. Neither clock extends a forced Spot interruption deadline.

## Read the configuration by owner

The same word can name several settings. `MinSize` on the group and `MinSize` on a warm pool are different parameters. Refresh warmup can override group warmup, while a policy can have another fallback chain. Inspect the exact path, owner, units, explicit or omitted state, effective source, and change timing before comparing values.

Changing a launch template reference affects future launches. It does not repaint existing instances as updated. A rolling refresh, a root-volume replacement, and a health replacement follow different eligibility and lifecycle rules even when they converge on the same desired template.

## Choose settings from the workload

Start with what makes capacity useful. A web service may care about registered targets, request latency, and safe connection draining. A queue worker may care about visible backlog per usable worker, message visibility timeout, long-job protection, and acknowledgment. A GPU workload may be constrained by a reservation or an instance type before any scaling policy gets a chance to help.

The queue fixture uses two seconds of average service time and 60 seconds of acceptable queue delay, which gives a starting target of 30 messages per worker. That relationship assumes comparable workers and service times; it is not a latency guarantee. At zero workers, the ratio is undefined and CPU samples may be absent, so an independent queue-depth signal must wake the fleet.

## Diagnose the first missing condition

Begin with the requested-versus-serving gap, then find the stage where progress stopped. Activity history can show that a launch request failed; EC2 state can show that an instance exists; target health can show why it is not routed; refresh status can show which readiness gate is still waiting. Those observations describe different moments in the path.

<ReferenceTable caption="Evidence narrows the failure domain" headers={['Observation', 'Check next', 'Why']} rows={[

  ['Warm starts succeed, then replacements loop', 'Pool depletion and the template used by cold launches', 'Prepared instances can hide a broken current launch path'],
  ['A Spot instance disappears during cleanup', 'Recommendation time, replacement readiness, drain and hook clocks', 'Protection and hooks cannot freeze forced reclamation'],

Repeated launch failures can lead to administrative process suspension. Treat that as evidence about the group’s recent failures, not as proof that the scaling policy is wrong. Keep observation timestamps visible: a missing metric sample is not zero, a pending instance is not a successful launch, and a healthy replacement does not automatically mean a rollout has passed bake time.

## Operations that look similar are not interchangeable

`EnterStandby` changes ASG membership state and begins deregistration. Directly deregistering a target does not put the instance in standby. Detaching removes ASG management while the EC2 instance continues; entering standby preserves group membership. Whether either operation decrements desired capacity determines whether the group asks for replacement capacity.

Scale-in protection applies to ordinary scale-in selection. EC2 termination protection, group deletion protection, refresh treatment of protected instances, and a forced Spot interruption are separate safeguards. When every active instance is protected, desired capacity can fall while actual capacity remains above it.

The nine suspendable processes also have narrow effects: `Launch`, `Terminate`, `AddToLoadBalancer`, `AlarmNotification`, `AZRebalance`, `HealthCheck`, `InstanceRefresh`, `ReplaceUnhealthy`, and `ScheduledActions`. Resuming a process does not universally replay everything missed while it was suspended. In particular, past scheduled actions are not replayed, and instances missed while `AddToLoadBalancer` was suspended need repair.
